01Principles

Five rules the whole design follows.

1. Calm under pressure

This is a 911 product. The interface should feel steady and serious: black ground, white type, very little ornament. Urgency is shown by state (a Critical badge, a red stripe), never by a busy or flashing layout.

2. One red, used with intent

The logo has exactly one colour. The UI keeps that discipline: crimson means "Nexus911" or "critical". It never decorates, and it never becomes a hover colour.

3. Every fact shows its evidence

The product's core idea is verification. The design makes it visible: confidence bars, source counts and "disputed" flags sit next to every fact, in the same position every time.

4. Two voices

A human serif for headlines and stories; a mono "machine" voice for labels, numbers and states. A plain sans carries everything in between. You can tell at a glance whether you're reading a person's words or system data.

5. Honest about being a prototype

Simulated callers, no real accounts and no real dispatch are stated plainly on the landing page, at sign-in and in onboarding. The design never implies more than the system does.

Why

Why only one red. The logo uses one colour, and a 911 product needs red to keep its meaning. If red also decorated links or hovers, a critical incident would stop standing out.

Why small labels are white, not red. #C00000 is a deep crimson: beautiful as a fill, but only 3.24:1 against black, which fails WCAG AA for small text. So the red moves into a shape (a dot, a rule, a filled button) and the words stay white. This keeps the brand visible and the text readable.

Why Critical shares the brand red. A second, brighter alert red would make two reds compete. Instead, Critical is always the filled crimson badge plus the word "Critical" plus a 3px left stripe on the incident card, so it never depends on colour alone. (See open decisions if this should change.)

Why green and amber stay. The logo has no state colours, and confidence needs them. Green and amber were kept from the existing Nexus911 dashboard so operators keep the meanings they already know.

Why

Why a serif in a 911 tool. The logo's "NEXUS" is a high-contrast serif, so the brand already has a human voice. Fraunces matches that feel and is a variable font, so one file covers every weight the dashboard uses.

Why mono for labels and numbers. Confidence values, source counts and timestamps line up in columns (tabular figures), and uppercase mono reads clearly as "system data". It separates what the machine measured from what a caller said.

Why the logo's own fonts aren't used. The exact logo typefaces aren't known. Fraunces and Plex are a stand-in that can be swapped in one place (the font variables) once the real names are confirmed.

Why

The logo is simple enough that its parts can be used on their own. Repeating the disc, the underline and the dot ties every screen back to the brand without adding a single new colour. Why

A dispatcher scans, they don't read. Hairline rows and generous space make the order of things obvious without heavy boxes. Removing shadows keeps the black ground truly black, which also makes the crimson read stronger.

07Components

The parts every screen is built from.

Buttons

Primary (crimson fill, white text) is the one main action per screen. Ghost (outline) is the second choice. Quiet (grey text) is for Back, Skip and Sign out: always available, never shouting. Disabled drops to 36% opacity and shows a not-allowed cursor.

Spec label

Step 2 of 3How to read it

Sits above every heading and tells you where you are. The key part is white with a crimson dot; the rest is grey.

Badges describe state (pill-shaped, mono). Only Critical is filled. Tags mark a recommendation (square, filled crimson). Every badge has a word in it; none is colour-only.

The product's signature. The value and source count are always printed beside the bar, so the bar's colour is a second signal, not the only one. Disputed facts get the △ glyph and a one-line reason.

Form field

Enter your name, or continue as a guest.

Mono uppercase label above, 3px-radius input, crimson border when invalid. The error text is white with a crimson ✕, says what to do, and the field gets focus on submit.

Step rail, choice card, segmented control

Finished steps are filled crimson with a tick; the current step gets a crimson ring and halo; upcoming steps are grey outlines. Selected cards show a crimson border plus halo and a filled tick. Segmented controls use a raised grey, not red, for the selected option, because a mode choice isn't a brand moment.

Why

Each component has exactly one place where crimson is allowed, so a screen never has more than a few red marks. That keeps the eye going to the primary action and to Critical.

09The four screens

Landing, sign in, onboarding, dashboard.

01 · Landing

Order: hero → problem → how it works → six novelties → pull quote → comparison → limitations → "How would you like to start?" → footer. The problem comes before the features so the reader knows why the features matter. Limitations sit near the end, in full, before the call to action, so nobody signs up under a false impression. The comparison table uses ● / △ / ✕ plus words, so it reads in greyscale.

02 · Sign in

Split screen: a brand story panel on the left (with the disc), the form on the right. The prototype has no accounts, so sign-in asks only for a name, kept in the browser, and says so in plain words. "Continue as guest" is a full-width button and guests get every feature. There's deliberately no password field: a password that isn't checked would look like security without being any.

03 · Onboarding

Three steps in BizzNex's wizard: what it does, how to read it, how to start. Step 2 teaches the confidence/disputed language using the exact components the dashboard uses, and carries the prototype notice. Step 3 offers the highway demo (text or voice) or the dashboard, and if the server is down it says so and still lets you in. Skip is on every step, Escape skips, and focus moves to each heading. It shows once per person and can be replayed from the dashboard.

04 · Dashboard

The existing Nexus911 dashboard keeps its layout and behaviour. It only gains the palette and three header controls: who is signed in (or "Guest"), Replay intro, and Sign out. In the mock, the dashboard screen is an approximation used to show those controls and the colour treatment.

10Accessibility

Readable, operable, and never colour-only.

  • All body text meets WCAG AA (4.5:1). Crimson is used as text only at large sizes, where 3:1 applies.
  • Every coloured state also has a glyph or word: ● Critical, △ Disputed, ✕ errors, the value and source count beside each bar.
  • Visible focus on every control: a 2px crimson outline (3.24:1, above the 3:1 minimum for UI).
  • Onboarding is a modal dialog: focus moves to each step's heading, Tab stays inside, Escape skips, arrow keys move between the start choices.
  • Form errors are linked to their field (aria-describedby, aria-invalid) and focus returns to the field.
  • The comparison table is a real table with row and column headers, scrolling sideways inside its own box on phones.
  • Reduced motion turns off all animation. Storage failures (private mode) never break the flow.

11Writing

Short, plain, and from the operator's side.

  • Say what a control does. "Run the demo", "Open the dashboard", "Replay intro". Not "Submit" or "OK".
  • Errors say how to fix it. "Enter your name, or continue as a guest." No apologies, no codes.
  • Name things the way people know them. "Confidence" and "sources", not "NLI entailment score".
  • State limits plainly. "The callers are simulated, and no real emergency services are contacted."
  • Headlines are short. Twenty characters or so: "Three callers. One incident."
  • Numbers are real. 240M calls, 25–30% vacancies and the highway-scenario details all come from the Nexus911 README and scenario files.
